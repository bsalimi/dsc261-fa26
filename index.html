Course description

Trustworthiness is a claim that must survive contact with a real system.

DSC 261 is an evidence-first technical course about evaluating, attacking, defending, inspecting, monitoring, and controlling deployed machine-learning systems. The course focuses on modern language models and agents, but the central discipline is broader: define the system boundary, state a falsifiable trust claim, choose valid evidence, and make an accountable decision.

Foundations are introduced only when a paper or experiment needs them. Most meetings center on primary research from leading venues and end with an executable object: an evaluation contract, metric, estimator, attack, defense, intervention, monitor, or control protocol.

1

Evidence before adjectives

“Safe,” “fair,” “robust,” and “private” are not labels. Students learn to scope each claim to a system, operating context, adversary, metric, threshold, and uncertainty statement.

2

Primary papers, used technically

Seminal and recent papers supply mechanisms, figures, estimators, experiments, and failure boundaries—not just discussion prompts.

3

One evidence-producing project

Teams investigate a real LLM system by measuring, auditing, attacking, defending, inspecting, monitoring, or controlling it.

Course format: instructor-led technical lectures, structured paper responses, hands-on problem sets, and a quarter-long project. There are no student paper presentations. Students present their own project and evidence at the end of the quarter.
Learning outcomes

What students should be able to do

Specify and evaluate

  • Reconstruct the relevant model, data, tools, permissions, and deployment boundary.
  • Translate a vague trust claim into scenarios, metrics, thresholds, intervals, and failure events.
  • Diagnose construct-validity, contamination, aggregation, and judge-bias failures.

Attack and defend

  • Implement or reproduce a scoped attack, audit, intervention, monitor, or control protocol.
  • Compare defenses under adaptive re-attack and account for utility costs.
  • Make a deployment recommendation whose evidence and limitations are explicit.
Prerequisites: machine learning, probability, linear algebra, and fluent Python. Specialized refreshers appear just in time; the course does not spend the quarter rebuilding general ML foundations.

Quarter-long work

The project is the spine of the course.

Small teams will make and test one technically precise trust claim about a real LLM system. A successful project produces evidence—not a survey—and ends with a reproducible artifact, a concise report, and an in-person project presentation.

Measure or audit

Design a valid evaluation, uncertainty study, privacy audit, fairness audit, attribution test, or monitor.

Attack or defend

Implement an adaptive red-team evaluation, poisoning experiment, prompt-injection defense, or robust-refusal protocol.

Inspect or control

Test an interpretability claim, intervention, agent sandbox, escalation rule, or adversarial control protocol.

Weeks 1–2

Question and system boundary

Form teams, identify the system and stakeholders, state the claim, define the operating context, and name the failure event.

Week 3

Project proposal

Submit a concise proposal with primary sources, threat model, variables, feasibility check, and collaboration plan.

Weeks 4–5

Evaluation contract and pilot

Freeze the scenarios, metrics, baselines, thresholds, and analysis plan; run a small pilot before scaling.

Week 7

Mid-quarter evidence review

Show an executable artifact and preliminary evidence; document negative results, validity threats, and necessary redesign.

Weeks 9–10

Final study and reproducibility package

Complete the study, preserve configurations and raw outputs, and prepare a defensible conclusion with explicit boundaries.

Finals week

Project presentations

Each team presents its question, method, strongest evidence, failure boundary, and deployment implication. The exact presentation schedule will be posted on Canvas.

No student paper presentations. Paper teaching remains instructor-led. Student presentation time is reserved for original project work and the evidence each team produced.

Working norms

Technical ambition with responsible boundaries

Responsible security research

Red-team, extraction, prompt-injection, and agent experiments must remain within approved systems, accounts, data, and rate limits. Do not target people or services outside the assignment scope.

Reproducibility

Preserve model and dataset versions, prompts, code, seeds, configurations, raw outputs, exclusion rules, and analysis decisions. A result without provenance is not a finished result.

Collaboration and attribution

Teams may divide work, but every member must understand the full claim and evidence. Cite papers, code, datasets, tools, and external assistance precisely.

Use of generative AI

AI tools may support course work when their use follows the assignment and Canvas policy. Students remain responsible for verification, disclosure, source accuracy, and every submitted claim.

Accessibility and accommodations: students should contact the UC San Diego Office for Students with Disabilities and notify the instructor through the established course process. The Canvas syllabus contains the controlling policy language.
